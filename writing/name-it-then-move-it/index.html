Systems note · 002

Name it, then move it Four clicks hand a live LeKiwi from a script to a learned policy.

We recorded a LeKiwi bench robot for 9 minutes 52 seconds without a cut: a scripted pick-and-place, then a SmolVLA policy loaded onto the robot and driving the same arm. Nothing restarts: not the LeRobot host, not the Cake resident, not the runtime session. The interesting part is not the policy. It is the chain of permission around it.

01The demo, in one take

The screen is the operator console.

Behind the screen sits a LeKiwi on a wooden bench: three omni wheels, five arm joints, one gripper, a wrist camera, a front camera that is switched off, and a small yellow block about seven centimetres in front of a green pad.

Between the first click and the last, the same robot does the same task two ways. Mission 1 is deterministic. Its runtime searches for the block, measures whether it can reach it, picks it up, checks the grip with three short lifts of 15, 28 and 8 millimetres, and then hands over to a scripted carry that rolls the base forward in two measured 5 cm pulses, lowers the block onto the pad and rolls back.

Mission 2 is learned. A Small Vision-Language-Action policy — SmolVLA — runs on the robot’s own CPU and chooses every arm target from the wrist image and the joint readings. It takes 20 chunks and 319 seconds to do what the script does in a little over three minutes.

The recording is uncut and in real time. Nothing between the two missions restarts.

02Two brains, and the thing between them

Most robot demos answer one question: can the policy do the task? This one is built to answer a second one: when a script, a learned policy and a person are three different authorities over the same arm, what binds them?

Five parts share the robot, and no single one of them is in charge.

Cake is the layer that makes ownership explicit and gated as transactions: a resident that hosts signed, versioned modules, admits them before they run, and moves a live slot from one generation to the next. A learned policy is one component among many; what it may see and what it may touch are decided outside it, in the resident control plane.

03The click that grants nothing until it names everything

Before the first click, the console is not idle. It reads:

NOT READY · ROBOT MOTION IS HELD

Never confirmed: nobody has confirmed that this is the robot, this resident session and this host. robot_session_not_confirmed

The button opens a dialog that says what the permission is for:

CONFIRM THE CONNECTED ROBOT

This grants permission to move for this resident session and this host instance only. A resident restart, a host restart (even one that reuses the pid), a re-created supervisor slot, or a resident that does not report the pinned build answering holds motion again.

robot LeKiwi bench
resident session 01a13091…
supervisor slot instance 6003298…
host spawn sequence 1
host pid on the robot 2371

☐ I have checked that this is the LeKiwi in front of me and that its host was started on purpose.

This is the least cinematic click in the demo and the one most worth copying. Permission is not a checkbox in a settings file, not a browser session, and not a “yes” that survives a restart. It is a lease on a named set of processes, and every event that could make the name mean something else — a resident restart, a host restart even with the same pid, a re-created supervisor slot, a different pinned build — revokes it.

Observe stays available while motion is held, because observing does not move anything. Start, Scan scene, Return arm and Try again stay off until the robot in front of the operator has been named.

04The mission that stops instead of improvising

The second click runs mission-19a339e153eb.

Mission 1’s stages are on screen: Find, Pick, Carry, Place, Return. The runtime owns them, and every transition is a measurement, not a schedule. When the search finds no block, it sweeps. When it finds one, it measures reachability before it moves.

That measurement has three outcomes, and only two of them are motion. If the block is reachable by arm alone, the mission picks. If it needs the base to advance, the mission requires the mobile_approach capability. And if that capability is not there, the mission stops at WAITING_FOR_CAPABILITY — with the same mission id and the same target id — and waits. It does not improvise a base move.

Earlier that day we watched the wait end the way it should: the mission stopped at the capability boundary, the console ran Activate on the prepared Plan, and seconds later the mission continued in the same runtime session, with nothing restarted. In this recording the capability is already active before the first Start, so the wait never appears; the pick does.

The arm moves over the block, aligns in small steps and closes on it at 2:08. The three lifts follow.

Then at 3:05, something more interesting than a success. The Carry row reads “Failed here” for about a second. Nothing crashed. With the block in its jaws, the runtime can no longer measure the pad in the wrist frame, and the base does not move blind. The console’s demo director stops the mission, and the runner’s scripted carry takes over from beside Cake: two 5 cm pulses measured by the wheels, the block lowered and released between the pad’s logos, the arm back, the base back.

So mission 1 has two ways to stop, and both are the point. One waits for authority; the other refuses to act without evidence.

05The click that changes the brain

The third click says Enable VLA. The console answers: “Loading the VLA module on the robot.”

“Module” is the console’s word for it. In this build the policy is already on the robot’s disk: the click sends one swap request to the VLA runner, a process beside the runtime, the Cake resident and the host. The runner loads the checkpoint, checks its hash against the envelope, runs one warm-up inference, opens its own links to the wrist camera and the host, and moves the arm to the policy’s start pose. No model file crosses the network, none of the three long-lived processes restarts, and the console stays connected.

The operator also puts the block back by hand, to about mission 1’s start spot. That is not a detail to hide. This demo does not claim the policy can pick from a place where the script refuses, and it does not dress a reset up as autonomy.

06Twelve seconds of thinking, two seconds of moving

The fourth click gives the same task to the policy. The screen says: “The VLA policy is driving the arm. Thinking. Chunk 12.”

Mission 2 takes 319 seconds and 20 chunks. Each chunk is one inference on the robot’s CPU, and on the runs we watched that day it took 12.0 to 12.4 seconds. Each chunk is at most 50 actions at 30 Hz: about 1.7 seconds of arm motion. Most of the episode is the arm holding still while the robot thinks.

Every action the policy emits passes a gate before a servo sees it:

  • six finite numbers, or the chunk is refused;
  • the wrist roll frozen at −96.75°, the value every recorded take held;
  • the four arm joints other than the wrist roll — shoulder pan, lift, elbow, wrist flex — smoothed with an exponential average (α = 0.35) from the pose read at the chunk start, with at most 5° of change per joint per tick;
  • targets clamped to the joint ranges of the 30 recorded takes, widened by 3°;
  • a target whose tool height, by forward kinematics, falls below −0.1345 m keeps the previous target instead.

The jaws are never the policy’s value. The policy asks for open or closed; the runtime decides, with a latch on the closed reading, a minimum hold of 30 ticks, and an open threshold that has to be met for four ticks in a row.

The grip check then does what mission 1 does: three short lifts. It scores retention as the similarity between two wrist frames taken at different heights: 0.94 here, passing at 0.5. That is a frame score, not a probability. An empty close scored 0.883 on 9 October, which is why the number alone is never the claim.

And during the whole episode, every command carries base velocity zero. The policy outputs arm and gripper targets only. It cannot drive the wheels — by construction, not by policy.

Then the set-down rule, the release, and the card: “Mission 2 complete. The VLA policy carried the block to the pad; grip confirmed; jaws opened by rule at the pad; 20 chunks in 319 s.”

07Who owns what

Read the demo as five owners, not one stack. The table below is that ownership in words.

PartRuns onOwnsDoes not own
LeRobot hostRobotMotor, servo and camera I/O; the ZMQ link; torque on connectMissions, policies, permission
Mission runtimeRobotThe mission: reachability, search, pick, grip check, set-down and pad-frame checksThe policy; the capability; permission to move
VLA runnerRobotThe checkpoint, inference, the action gate, the dead-man, zero base velocityThe mission; Cake; the wheels
Cake resident + mobile_approachRobotAdmission and lifecycle of the capability; the activation transaction; session, generation and epoch readbackThe script; the policy; torque
Operator consoleDesktopFour buttons, and the evidence it reads backAny authority of its own

Two things in that table deserve repeating. The capability Cake holds was required by mission 1, but in this scene it never moves the robot: every mission 1 that day stopped at the hold before a base move of its own, and the rolling you see is the scripted carry, which runs beside Cake. And the VLA load is a runner operation, not a Cake transaction. Both are honest limits, and both are visible on the screen.

08The panel that shows its sources

The panel on the right is where the demo earns its keep. It shows what Cake holds, what that was checked against, and — where the evidence is older — it says so. In the recording it reads:

CAKE ON THE ROBOT · OLDEST FIRST

No Cake activation in this mission.

SIGNATURE CHECK: EARLIER SESSION
No check in this runtime session. The last one, 2026-10-11 15:12:24Z (runtime session rt-20261011t150926Z-50abac15), is in History.

LIVE SLOT
mobile_approach, active, generation 2, capsule 2f34a84c1971, as the robot’s reading says.
No record of this mission or of this resident session says what set it.

Read the last two lines together. The console knows the slot is active; it read that from the robot. It also knows that nothing in this session says what made it active, and it says so instead of inventing a cause. The signature check is dated and attributed to an earlier session rather than borrowed. The activation history is cards, oldest first, so a refusal stays visible after an activation replaces it.

Under that panel is the machinery this demo is really showing:

  • A Plan names slots, typed bindings, resource grants and content identities, and is hashed.
  • A capsule is a signed module package. It is admitted at start under a signature policy, and its content identity is re-hashed on every store read.
  • Activation is a transaction: compute the closure, drain the retiring generation, rebind under an epoch guard, publish the new binding snapshot, reclaim. A candidate that fails validation is refused before any effect, with the slot’s generation unchanged.
  • The readback is polled, not pushed: session identity, build, Plan digest, epoch, slot generation, lifecycle state, health and a flight ring of typed events.

Two Plans were prepared for this demo: one to activate, and one deliberately outside what Cake will admit, to show a refusal as a refusal. The console has a card shape for each.

One boundary, because it always belongs here: Cake’s modules run in one address space. The kernel guarantees the transaction, not the fault. A bad write inside a capsule can still take the session with it.

09What this demo does not claim

The claims are narrow on purpose. Four limits are worth stating.

  • The block reached the pad in two of the six VLA runs on record that day, and where it lands is not measured: the jaws open by rule wherever the latched arm comes to rest in the end-of-take region.
  • Mission 1 is tuned for one spot: about 3 cm farther and the arm runs out of reach, 4 cm nearer and the search refuses the grasp. The carry is scripted, and measured by the wheels.
  • This recording shows no Cake activation and no Cake module replacement: the capability was already active before the first Start. The lifecycle beats are in the other demo in the same repository.
  • Actuator-safe recovery is not demonstrated: the host re-enables torque on every connect, and Cake has no torque concept. The policy also runs at about 12 seconds per chunk on the Pi’s CPU.

10Reproduce it, or read what it rests on

The demo, the runner and the record are in the kiwi-cake-demo repository, on the branch publish/vla-four-click-demo-20261013 (revision 8e521cf). The recording is embedded in its README; the run-by-run record is four-click-demo-2026-10-12.md; the runner is vla/README.md; and the capability assessment is cake-capability-assessment.md.

If you want the lifecycle demo without a robot, the same repository’s main branch runs a harmless stub on a Pi 5 and lets you break it on purpose: change one byte of the stored capsule and it refuses to start, kill the child and it restarts, kill the resident and it comes back with a fresh session and byte-identical declared identities. That is the older, less photogenic half of the same story.

Cake’s live policy replacement is exercised in the Microduck note; this demo is about the authority around a policy that runs beside it.

11The takeaway

A demo like this is easy to make impressive and hard to make trustworthy. The impressive part is a policy that picks up a block. The trustworthy part is everything that can say no: no motion until the robot, the session and the host are named; no base move without the capability; no carry without a pad frame; no action outside the gate; no claim about a slot that this session cannot source.

That is what we wanted to show. Not that a small VLA can pick up a block (it can, slowly, on a Pi), but that the deployment can say exactly what happened, exactly what did not, and exactly what it does not know.

The robot did not restart to gain a capability. It did not pretend the policy was one.
