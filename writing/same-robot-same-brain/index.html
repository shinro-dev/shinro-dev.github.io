Systems note · 001

Change a walking robot's software without stopping it Any part, swapped mid-stride.

You can replace a walking robot's brain while it is walking. Not pause it, not hold it up: the part that decides where it goes and the brain that keeps it upright can both be swapped mid-stride, and the duck carries on. The program that does the swapping is Cake, and Microduck, the small open-source duck from Pollen Robotics, is where you can watch it.

No robot, no GPU, about two minutes: Try it yourself · the demo on GitHub ↗

It is a 25 cm duck that walks on two legs and 14 motors. Everything below runs in a simulator, on an Intel or AMD Linux machine.

01The problem: you can't pause a walking robot

A walking robot needs its software running every moment, even to stand still. In its standing pose, Microduck's centre of gravity sits about 5 mm in front of its ankles. Its walking brain, a small neural network, keeps it balanced by correcting its legs 50 times a second.

That brain is only one part of the robot's software. Another one, the navigator, decides where to go: a circle, a figure 8, a slalom. Over a robot's life you keep changing these parts: a new route, a better-trained brain, a bug fix.

The simple way to change a part is to stop the robot's software and start it again. On a walking robot, that stops the walk, and stopping mid-step can make it fall.

Microduck's own software avoids this for its brains. It keeps one per move, from walking to roller skating, and switches between them while the duck runs. It can even load a new walking brain from the Hugging Face Hub without restarting. So the real question is: can every part be changed like that, while the others keep the robot on its feet?

02The solution: give each part its own slot

Cake runs each part of a robot's software as a separate module, in its own slot. While the robot walks, one command switches a slot from one module to another, and the other modules keep running.

Microduck's own software already swaps its walking brain. What Cake adds is that the slot isn't reserved for brains: anything with a declared set of inputs and outputs can sit in it, a navigator now, a filter or a behaviour someone writes later. That's the difference between shipping a robot and shipping one you can maintain: a route change, a better-trained brain or a bug fix goes in while the duck keeps working, and a bad brain comes back out the same way. Each part ships as one small self-contained program, so there's nothing else to install on the robot.

Pollen's runtime can swap its brains too, but it walks the robot home first, as its policy-channel notes say. With Cake, no such return happens during the policy swap. It's the difference between a service that goes down for maintenance and one that doesn't.

Key takeaways from the demo:

  • One slot per part. The walking brain sits in one slot and the navigator in another, each running on its own.
  • Prepared before the robot moves. When Cake starts, it loads every module you may switch to and checks that their inputs and outputs fit together.
  • Swap one, the rest keep going. Cake winds down the old module, connects the new one in its slot, then unloads the old one. The brain keeps running throughout.
  • Nothing restarts. Cake reports the same session ID before and after the swaps, so you can check for yourself that it never restarted.

Think of a long drive: you can change who reads the map without stopping the car, as long as the driver keeps driving. Here the walking brain drives and the navigator reads the map.

Swapping the brain itself is harder, because in the simple setup the brain also keeps the clock and holds the link to the robot. So for that demo, those two jobs move into a small module that never changes, the bridge. The bridge then blends from the old brain to the new one over half a second.

03Demo A: one walking brain for another

This is the comparison that matters. We swapped Pollen's default walking brain for a different walking brain while the robot was moving, and ran the same two brains, at the same requested speed, on Pollen's own runtime.

Same robot, same two walking brains, same speed asked for. Ours (green) never stops: 1.1 m from where it started. Theirs (amber) gets 15 cm, and the flat middle of the chart is the 1.4 seconds it spent on the floor: their swap landed too, and Pollen's own velstand command is what stood the duck back up. Both swaps worked. Only one kept the duck walking.

Both stacks do the swap, and neither restarts the software: theirs accepted the new walking brain mid-walk, and so did ours. The difference is what happens to the duck in between. On their runtime, loading a walking brain means walking the duck back to its standing pose first; from the middle of a step, that ends with it on the floor. Cake changes the brain underneath the running loop instead, so the duck notices nothing except a slightly different gait, and carries on for the remaining 1.1 m.

Ten runs on an x86 machine back this up. All ten swaps completed. The session ID was identical before and after, the duck stayed upright, and the loop never missed a tick: 515 to 518 steps, every interval exactly 20 ms. The gait does change at the swap: peak tilt rises from about 5° to 7° for half a second, and then settles again.

04Demo B: swapping the navigation target

The navigator is the part that decides where to go. In this run Cake replaces it twice while the duck walks, from a circle to a figure 8 and then to a slalom, with each of those loaded as a spare before the robot moved.

Each navigator steers toward its own shape. In 8 seconds the duck walks only part of it, so the path bends a different way after each swap, and the line never breaks. In the 24-second run, the walking brain sent the simulator 1,212 commands, about 50 a second. The duck covered about 2.7 m along its path, measured every half second so the side-to-side sway of each step doesn't count. Cake reported the same session ID before and after both swaps.

Across 4 runs, Cake swapped the navigator 8 times while the duck walked, and the duck never stopped or fell.

05Demo C: swapping the brain itself

Microduck's own software swaps its brains too, on the real robot. Here Cake does it in simulation, with the same command it used for the navigator, and the duck changes state: standing to walking, and walking to standing.

After the swap from standing to walking, the duck reached its walking speed in under 1.5 seconds. The other direction is rougher: in Fig. 5 the duck lurched forward at about twice its walking speed before standing still, about 1.5 seconds after the swap. The standing brain expects a robot that is already still, so the hand-over is not smooth. In our other runs the lurch was smaller, and the duck stayed on its feet in all 6.

06What this demo does not show

  • A real robot. Everything runs in a simulator, and we have not connected Cake to a real Microduck or to its own software. The duck's own computer uses an ARM chip, a Rockchip RK3566, and this demo's Cake release only runs on Intel and AMD computers.
  • Real-world timing. In the navigator demo, the simulator waits for each command from Cake, so a slow swap would only pause the walk. A real robot does not wait. This demo does not measure how long a swap takes.
  • Your own parts. This release runs only the modules Shinro built, all loaded when Cake starts. You can't build your own module with it, or add one to a running Cake.

07Try it yourself

On our 2-vCPU Linux cloud machine it took about 2 minutes from clone to first result, most of it installing about 170 MB of Python packages. You need no GPU, no screen and no robot.

What you need

  • Linux on an Intel or AMD 64-bit processor, with glibc 2.34 or newer (check with ldd --version), such as Ubuntu 24.04.
  • python3 version 3.12 or newer with its venv module (on Ubuntu, the python3-venv package), plus git, make and curl.

The Cake programs in this demo are only published for Intel and AMD Linux, so Macs and ARM boards can't run them.

1. Install everything (about 1.5 minutes)

shell

git clone https://github.com/Shinro-xyz/shinro-demo-microduck.git
cd shinro-demo-microduck
make venv
make install PYTHON=.venv/bin/python
make fetch-cake PYTHON=.venv/bin/python

make venv creates a separate Python environment for the demo, so nothing is installed system-wide.

2. Swap the navigator while the duck walks (about 25 seconds)

shell

make cake PYTHON=.venv/bin/python GIF=

The duck walks for 24 seconds, and Cake swaps its navigator at 8 and 16 seconds. The run ends like this (trimmed; your session ID will differ):

output

microduck-cake: swapping trajectory -> eight
microduck-cake: transaction_phase COMPLETED
microduck-cake: swapping trajectory -> slalom
microduck-cake: transaction_phase COMPLETED
microduck-cake: session unchanged (01a16ac778917f2881bb3a809fa62db0); trajectory slot unchanged
walk: steps=1212 travel=1.732 m speed=0.071 m/s tilt=1.7 deg z=0.117 m
microduck-cake: microduck Cake demo: PASS

COMPLETED means the swap went through, and session unchanged means Cake never restarted. steps=1212 is the number of commands the simulator received: about 50 a second for 24 seconds. tilt and z say the duck ended upright. travel and speed use the straight line from start to finish, so they read lower than the distance and speed along the path.

3. Swap the brain (about 12 seconds per run)

shell

make fetch-cake-policy-swap PYTHON=.venv/bin/python
make policy-swap PYTHON=.venv/bin/python PGIF=
make policy-swap PYTHON=.venv/bin/python PGIF= FROM=walk TO=stand

The duck starts on the standing brain, and Cake swaps in the walking one after 2 seconds. The last command goes the other way. Each run ends with a PASS line.

4. Swap one walking brain for another (about 12 seconds per run)

shell

make fetch-cake-walk-swap PYTHON=.venv/bin/python
make walk-swap PYTHON=.venv/bin/python PGIF=

This is the comparison in Demo A: the same duck, the same request to walk, with a second walking brain swapped in mid-walk instead of the standing one. Each run ends with a PASS line, and the duck keeps walking through the swap.

5. Look at the data

Each run writes its files to state/run/ (navigator) or state/run-policy-swap/ (brain and walking-brain swaps): trace.csv, the duck's position 50 times a second, and a record of each swap, swaps.json or policy_swap.json. The two charts above were drawn from these files. A new run of the same command deletes the previous run's files, so copy them first if you want to keep them.

Why GIF=?

It turns off the animated video. On our 2-vCPU machine, recording it slowed the navigator run to about a third of real time and dropped the duck in all 4 brain-swap runs. On a faster computer, try without GIF= and PGIF= to get an animation in state/media/. If the brain swap then fails, put them back.
